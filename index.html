Why I Wrote This Book

I saw a gap the curriculum was not filling, and the people who needed it most were already telling me what they wanted.

The need I kept seeing

Over the past few years I have mentored more than 40 fourth-year capstone projects and trained more than 12,000 students, and a pattern kept repeating. Once the technical work was done and the demo was over, students would stay behind and ask the questions the curriculum didn’t cover: how to choose a first job, how to recover from a failure, how to become someone others want to follow.

I found myself giving the same answers again and again, one student at a time. That told me two things: the need was real and widely shared, and one-to-one advice would never reach everyone who needed it. So I decided to write the answers down properly, test them against the evidence, and give them away.

What it took

  • 40+fourth-year capstone projects mentored
  • 12,000+students trained
  • 37+drafts of the manuscript; I stopped counting after the 37th
  • ~200research papers read, by my estimate, to gather the evidence

I also interviewed executives from multiple industries to understand how they operate the most complex systems they run: their companies.

Then I made sure it could reach the people it was written for. I secured the permission of my mentor, Strauss Zelnick, to share the series freely; published it open access with a DOI; and deposited it in the University of Ottawa’s institutional repository after review by the University Library’s Copyright Office.

What students needed, and how the book answers it

A framework they already trust
A career framed the way engineering students already think: as a control system. Set the target, measure honestly, correct the drift, close the loop.
Advice from people who have done it
Strauss Zelnick’s Success: A Concise Guide to Having the Life You Want, adapted to engineering careers and read alongside Morse, Schell and Babcock’s Managing Engineering and Technology. Shared with his permission; that permission is not an endorsement.
Practice, not just theory
A case method inspired by Harvard Business School’s teaching tools, which build leadership by analysing real-life challenges; here, fictional but realistic engineering cases. Every case puts students in someone else’s shoes and asks one question: If you were that person, what would you do?
Claims they can rely on
Arguments are tested against peer-reviewed studies and reports, including where the research complicates or corrects them.
Something instructors can teach tomorrow
An introduction, nine lectures and a conclusion, each with a case study, discussion questions, assignments and references, so it can be taught one lecture at a time.

If you teach, mentor or hire engineers, I would value your advice on how to make it better, and you are welcome to share it with your students. Write to me. — D! (Puranjan Gaur)

About the Book

Nine short lectures on how engineering careers actually go, and what a graduating engineer can do about it, framed by an introduction and a conclusion.

The series was written for senior capstone students in computer, electrical, mechatronics, mechanical and software engineering. It assumes you can read a block diagram, argue about a tolerance, and tell the difference between an estimate and a measurement, and it uses exactly those habits: it treats a career as a closed-loop control system, with a setpoint, a controller, a plant, sensors, feedforward and an interlock.

Every lecture is built around a fictional engineering case with a real decision in it: the case comes straight after the short opening chapter, and the last chapter returns to it. Between the two sit three concept chapters, each ending in an assignment; across the three assignments you build one short working document of your own, such as a Personal Setpoint, a Launch Plan or a Proposal Kit.

The case that runs through the series

The Northline Programme. Northline Energy Systems, an invented company, builds containerised battery storage and microgrid controllers for remote northern communities and mine sites that run on diesel. The case follows Amara Osei, a new electrical-engineering graduate with two job offers and a funded graduate place, from 2027 to 2034.

It is deliberately unresolved: nothing in the book says what she should do. The Introduction sets it out and maps every lecture onto it, and the Conclusion works through eight of its decision points.

2.1 Three sources, and what each is for

The narrative frame

Strauss Zelnick, Success: A Concise Guide to Having the Life You Want, 2nd ed. (2015). Zelnick was president of Twentieth Century Fox at thirty-two, later chief executive of BMG Entertainment, co-founded the investment firm ZMC, and is today chairman and chief executive of Take-Two Interactive. For decades he has advised and mentored people starting their careers, and the book distils that advice.

The engineering context

Lucy C. Morse, William J. Schell IV and Daniel L. Babcock, Managing Engineering and Technology, 7th ed. (Pearson, 2020): how careers in engineering are structured, which career fields a degree opens, and how professionals define success for themselves.

The evidence

Peer-reviewed studies and reports test both. Where the research supports Zelnick you see the study; where it complicates or corrects him, you see that too. About 200 distinct sources are cited across the eleven parts. You are meant to argue with this material, not memorise it.

The Engineer’s Roadmap

A career as a closed-loop control system: what each block is, and the lecture that develops it

Highlight a lecture:

No block selected. Select a block in the chart, or a row in the table below.

How to read the chart

  • Forward path. Setpoint, controller (what you decide), interlock, plant (your work, your team, your reputation).
  • Labels. L1–L9 on each block mark the lecture(s) that develop it. The blocks are separable, and each can be improved on its own.
  • Comparator. Subtracts what you measure (−) from the setpoint (+); the difference is the error the controller acts on.
  • Fast loop. The sensor feeds the comparator directly: what you can measure this week — tests, numbers, people telling you plainly.
  • Slow loop. What you do reaches your standing only after dead time (months for a reputation, years for a standing), is estimated by the observer (violet paths), and revises the setpoint through the governor (garnet path), on purpose rather than by drift. The observer also takes in what you actually did (violet dashed line from the controller) and informs the controller.
  • Disturbances and feedforward. Disturbances act on the results whatever you do. Feedforward acts on the part you can see coming (orange dashed path) — savings, a current résumé, a network — before the error appears.
  • Integrity interlock. In series with everything that reaches the plant, feedforward included. It is not a weighting inside the controller: it is normally closed, and either trip opens it and stops the process — the first trip from the integrity test of the decision on the table (red dashed path running up from the controller), or the second trip from the watchdog, which checks what it is costing you.

After a Failure: A Decision Chart

Success as an Engineer — Professional Practice Series

What kind of failure is this, and what do you do next?

University of Ottawa · Faculty of Engineering · Puranjan Gaur

A one-page companion to Lecture 6 (Integrity, Truth and Mistakes) and Lecture 9 (Renewal), placed inside Lecture 6 after Chapter 5, When You Make Mistakes (page 231). It is adapted from the author’s own reflection on failure:

LinkedIn is full of success; few people talk about the failures that came first. Why is it so hard to acknowledge that we failed many times before getting what we wanted? After a good deal of success, for which I am grateful, I looked back at my career and at my personal, social, health and mental struggles. This chart sets out what I concluded, for anyone learning to overcome outcomes they did not want.

An outcome you wanted did not happen

AAcknowledge that you failed, without blaming others.

Q1Can you overcome it or recover from it?

Yes: follow the teal steps below.No: skip to the violet steps (U1–U4).

Q1 answered Yes: can recover

Q2Short term or long term?

Decide by your situation, not the exact period.

Long term: the same steps, with extreme patience and discipline

Short term: go to R1. Long term: the same steps, R1 to R4, with extreme patience and discipline. Decide by your situation, not the exact period.

R1Understand what actions led to the failure.

R2Hypothesize. Introspect: which actions might help you recover? Act on them.

Q3Is your hypothesis valid?

If no: introspect again and return to R2. If yes: go to R3.

R3Make deliberate choices and take every necessary action to get back on track.

R4Once back on track, make mental notes of what worked and what didn’t.

Recovered, with the lessons

Q1 answered No: cannot recover

U1Take every necessary action to minimize the impact.Failed to deliver a project at work? Don’t come home and talk to your parents as if it were their fault.

U2Make mental notes. Ask yourself:

  • Why did I fail?
  • What could have been done to prevent it?
  • What could have been done to improve the situation, context or environment when it happened?

U3Forgive yourself and move on.

U4Apply the lessons in other situations in your life, so you don’t end up here again.

Moved on, with the lessons

How to read the chart

  • Pill shapes: where you start and finish.
  • Diamonds: questions. Q1 and Q2 sort the failure; Q3 checks your hypothesis.
  • Blue boxes (diamonds in the original chart): questions. Q1 and Q2 sort the failure; Q3 checks your hypothesis.
  • Boxes: actions. A: the shared first step; R: if you can recover; U: if you cannot.
  • Dashed band: the long-term route (R1–R4).

Recover or not, every path begins with honest acknowledgement and ends with lessons learned.
